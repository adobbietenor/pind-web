# M4.4, as Alex actually wants it — the answers before the rescope

**Status: answers only. Nothing is rescoped and nothing is built.** Alex asked five
questions after the M2.3 walk (20 Sept 2026) and said he would decide whether M4.4
moves once they were answered. M4.4 currently sits after the crew loop at 8–12 hours
(`docs/build-plan.md` §8), and the reason it sits there has not changed: the loop is
the untested risk.

**What he asked for, in his words:** "A run every day or two that searches for Toronto
community gatherings, categorises and tags them, and auto-publishes the good ones into
the upcoming list. Not a weekly batch I review — the same automate-by-default standard
as the Ticketmaster import: AI does the work, I remove mistakes."

That is a different machine from the one in the plan. The plan has a weekly AI search
producing a review queue. What he wants is **a second importer**, with a different fetch
layer and its own rubric, running on its own cron, publishing through the same door.

---

## 1 · What "find things" means when there is no API

**Both, but they are two different jobs on two different clocks, and conflating them is
what makes this expensive and unreliable.**

The manual pass is the evidence. What produced real, dated, confirmable gatherings was
**enumerable institutions**: Toronto Public Library's per-branch event pages (which is
how Port Union and Kennedy/Eglinton were confirmed) and the City's own drop-in sports
map. What produced almost nothing was searching for things to do in Toronto. Those two
sources are not directories and no "what's on in Toronto" search reaches them — they are
per-location pages that have to be enumerated (decisions Part 5, "The doughnut is a
sourcing problem").

So:

- **The daily run works from a maintained source list**, and it does not search. Each
  entry is a URL and an adapter: fetch the page, extract dated occurrences, compare with
  what we already have. That is cheap, fast, repeatable, and its failures are legible —
  a page that changed shape produces zero events and says so, rather than a model
  quietly inventing three.
- **A separate, much rarer pass proposes new *sources*, never events.** Claude with web
  search, once a week or once a month, asked "what organisations in Toronto publish their
  own dated listings for X" — and its output is a candidate *source* for Alex to accept
  into the list, with the page it found. One accepted source then feeds the daily run
  forever. This is the part that scales to a second city, which is the stated goal:
  "a city can be sourced", not "six venues are kept up to date".

  **This is a rule, not an implementation detail** (Alex, on the record, 20 Sept 2026).
  The proposal pass may output a source and nothing else. **If it ever starts proposing
  gatherings directly, it has become the expensive unreliable thing this design
  rejected** — 43 s to 219 s a call, empty answers, mid-stream stalls, $0.19 a go, and
  no page to re-check next week. The tell is easy to watch for: a proposed row that has
  a date on it rather than a URL to enumerate. A source is durable and gets re-read every
  night; an event found by search is a one-off assertion nobody can verify tomorrow.

**Why this split matters more than it looks.** M1.3b measured what AI web search costs
and how it behaves: 43 s for one venue and 219 s for another, empty answers, mid-stream
stalls, one run hung for forty minutes, about $0.19 a call. That is tolerable monthly
and intolerable daily. A source list turns the daily job into ordinary HTTP plus a small
extraction call, which is the difference between a job that can run every night and one
that cannot.

**What the source list is, concretely.** A table — `community_sources` — with the URL,
an adapter name, the organisation, a cadence hint, when it was last fetched, what it
produced, and whether Alex has confirmed it. It is the same shape as
`venue_external_ids` and `import_runs`: the thing that makes a nightly job auditable is
a row per attempt.

## 2 · The rubric, which cannot be the Ticketmaster one

**Comedy is the proof, and it happened in this milestone.** One rubric written about
arena events judged stand-up as seated theatre and refused a whole category: 48 drafts
at a median of 35 against a floor of 60, three of them ever publishable, and **nothing
anywhere said so** — the chip simply never appeared. Pointing that same rubric at a run
club would do the same thing more severely, because a run club fails almost every
criterion it scores on: crowd size (0–30) is twelve people, and "time and place to meet
before" is meaningless when the gathering *is* the meeting point.

So the community run scores on what makes a community gathering work for Pin'd:

1. **Solo-friendly by design, 0–30.** Can one person turn up alone and be fine? A
   drop-in games night and a run club are the top of this; a league needing a team, a
   couples' class and anything requiring a partner are the bottom. This is the criterion
   that replaces crowd size, and it is the one the whole product rests on.
2. **Big enough to make a crew, 0–20.** Not "big": **ten**. Crews open at five opted in
   and §7 expects opt-in at about half of pinners, so a room that cannot hold ten cannot
   produce five at any conversion rate (decisions, "Capacity: a room too small is a page
   the product cannot serve"). Above ten this criterion is about how reliably it fills,
   not how large it is.
3. **Recurring, 0–20.** A weekly fixture is worth more than a one-off festival, because
   a reader who misses it can go next week and because a crew that forms once can form
   again. This inverts the Ticketmaster rubric, where every listing is a one-off.
4. **Cheap or free to walk in, 0–15**, with the door price stated. Never inferred:
   "inferring free when it is actually $20 at the door is the most damaging mistake it
   can make" (decisions, M4.4), so an unknown price scores as unknown and does not
   silently score as free.
5. **Somewhere to convene, or no need for one, 0–15.** A gathering that is its own
   meeting point (`at_the_gathering`) scores full: nothing has to be arranged. One that
   needs a spot scores on whether a spot exists near it.

**Hard caps, by analogy with the ones that work:** anything whose audience is mostly
under 19, at most 10 (H8). Anything requiring membership, an audition or a team, at most
20. Anything whose next date could not be confirmed **is not scored at all** — it is
held, which is question 3.

**And the rubric is measured before it ships**, twice, with a control set, the way the
comedy change was: run it over the hand-entered gatherings — which are the known-good
answers — and check it agrees with the hand decisions before it is pointed at anything
new. **The seven Alex held are the labelled negatives**, and they are the more valuable
half: a rubric that publishes all 31 has learned nothing. That is the strongest thing
the manual pass left behind, and comedy is the proof of what the alternative costs —
**one rubric judging something it was not written about fails silently**, with no error
anywhere, for as long as nobody counts what it refused.

## 3 · Auto-publishing when verification is the hard part

**This is the real question, and the answer is that verification is not a score — it is
a separate gate with its own evidence, and only one grade of evidence publishes itself.**

The manual pass already invented the grades and used them: **[V]** confirmed on the
organiser's own page, **[D]** third-party directory only, **[?]** not confirmed, **[G]**
hours from a Google listing. Three of ten were held on those marks and one was dropped.
"A [?] is a reason to hold, not to publish with a caveat" is already decided.

So, as a rule:

- **Auto-publishes** only a gathering that is **[V]**: the next dated occurrence was
  found **on the organiser's own page** in this run, the entry cost is stated on that
  page, and capacity is either unknown or at least `cities.min_capacity`. Plus every
  automatic rule the publisher already applies — the floor, the caps, the lead window.
- **Queues for Alex** anything [D], [?] or [G]; anything whose capacity is known and
  below the floor; anything whose price the page does not state; anything the extractor
  produced with a missing field. The queue is the admin's Community tab, one line each
  with the source page and what was missing — **the refusal is as visible as the
  choice** (Alex, M2.2).
- **Never invents a date.** The most damaging output here is not a wrong score, it is a
  confident next occurrence for a series that has stopped. Which is the other half:

**The liveness check is the part the current plan does not have at all, and it is the
one that matters most.** A defunct run club on the site is worse than a thin list —
Alex's words — and the failure arrives *after* publication, not at discovery. The
Ticketmaster importer already has exactly this machinery and its rules are decided
(decisions, "Importer and status changes"):

> On a draft, the importer may update a changed date or quietly dismiss it when it is
> missing two nights running. On a **published** gathering it changes nothing: it raises
> a **flag**, and Alex decides.

Point that at community sources and it does the right thing: a series whose page stops
listing dates raises a flag on its upcoming occurrences rather than deleting them or
leaving them to rot. **A dismissed-by-the-source draft restores itself if the page comes
back; one Alex dismissed never does.** No new concepts.

**One thing to notice today, before M4.4 exists:** the recurrence generator has already
written **188 dated rows to mid-November** from 31 hand-entered series, and **nothing
checks whether any of them is still real.** That risk is live now and is independent of
the discovery half — see "What could move early".

## 4 · Recurrence, capacity and registration

All three are already in the schema, and all three came out of the manual pass, so the
run has to fill them rather than work around them.

- **Recurrence: the run must not invent a recurrence concept, because there isn't one.**
  The generator turns weekly, fortnightly and monthly into N dated drafts, monthly reads
  "last Sunday" correctly, and **there is deliberately no recurrence in the schema**. So
  the extractor's job is to produce *dated occurrences*, and where a page states a
  cadence the run uses the existing generator. The rule that must survive: **a series
  whose venue changes每 week is not a series** — Toronto Bike Network starts somewhere
  different every Saturday, and those are separate gatherings that share a name. The
  generator has no per-occurrence venue override on purpose.
- **Capacity** (`gatherings.capacity`, `cities.min_capacity`) is a publishing rule, not
  a caveat: known and under ten holds the gathering. **Places remaining is deliberately
  not modelled** and the run must not try — "3 spaces left" is a lie within the hour.
  The known edge is already written down: the James Gardens walk holds 15, clears the
  floor, and probably has no places left. When it bites, the argument has been had.
- **Registration** (`signup_url`, `signup_required`) is a line above the button, and a
  gathering that registers elsewhere is still pinnable, because "Pin in" is a statement
  about the person. The extractor must set it when the page says to register, and **must
  not** treat a registration page as a confirmation that the event is on — those are
  different facts.
- **Entry** (`entry`, `door_price_cents`, `entry_note`) with the rule already decided:
  never say free unless it is free; unknown reads "pay at the door" with no amount.

**Where community gatherings differ from everything M1.3 built**, and what M4.4
inherits rather than invents: the convening is a property of the gathering
(`at_the_gathering` / `a_spot_first` / `after`, default `at_the_gathering` for
Community, built in M3.3), and **community gatherings mostly need no meeting spots at
all**. That removes the thing the first research pass wasted its time on — three spots
for gatherings that need none.

## 5 · What daily costs, against the $3 cap

Measured numbers we already have: Ticketmaster scoring is **740 drafts for $0.77**, so
about **$0.001 a draft**, at 25 per call with `effort: low`. AI web search is
**$0.19–0.20 per call** and 60–100k input tokens, because the search results are the
input (M1.3b).

| shape | per day | why |
|---|---|---|
| **fetch + extract, 30 source pages** | **$0.30–0.90** | our own HTTP is free; each page is 3–15k tokens of text at $2/M input, plus a small structured answer |
| scoring whatever came out, ~20 new drafts | **$0.02** | the same call shape as Ticketmaster's, and the same price |
| **open-ended web search, 10 queries** | **$1.90** | $0.01 a search plus 60–100k input tokens a call |

So: **the daily run as designed costs well under a dollar and fits beside the
Ticketmaster import inside the $3 cap.** The same run built on web search does not — it
would take two thirds of the cap on its own, every day, for the least reliable part of
the pipeline. That is the arithmetic behind answer 1.

Two ops points that follow:
- **It needs its own cron and its own budget line**, not a step inside the nightly
  import. Long AI work in the import is already a known mistake (CLAUDE.md), and a
  community run that stalls must not delay publishing.
- **The cap must be per-job, not just per-day**, or a bad community night eats the
  budget the Ticketmaster scoring needs. `AI_DAILY_CAP_USD` becomes a cap with a split,
  or each job carries its own ceiling and the day's total is still enforced.

## What M4.4 becomes

**A second importer**, with the same skeleton as M1.3 and a different front end:

| piece | hours | note |
|---|---|---|
| `community` source value, `community_sources` table, run records | 1–2 | trivial, and it is what makes the rest auditable |
| fetch + extract: 3–4 adapters (TPL branches, City drop-in, organiser pages, one generic) | 6–10 | the bulk; a strict output schema, and zero events is a reported outcome, never a guess |
| the community rubric, scored and measured against the hand-entered 31 | 3–4 | the labelled test set already exists |
| liveness: flags on published occurrences, self-restoring drafts | 3–4 | reuses M1.3's rules wholesale |
| admin: Community tab, source pages, confidence marks, the hold queue with reasons | 4–6 | "why not" as visible as "why" |
| non-building venues, and the convening default | 2–3 | overlaps M3.3 |
| its own cron, its own budget line, the cap split | 1–2 | |
| **total** | **20–31 h** | against the 8–12 h now in the plan |
| *the weekly source-discovery pass (search), separately* | *3–5 h* | *can land later; the daily run works without it* |

So it roughly **doubles to triples**, and the increase is almost entirely the two things
the current scope does not have: **enumerable fetching instead of search**, and
**liveness after publication**. Both are what "auto-publishes the good ones" actually
requires; the weekly-batch-for-review version is cheaper because it makes Alex the
liveness check.

## What I would do about the order

**Leave M4.4 where it is.** The loop is still the risk, and nothing here changes that.
Two caveats worth having on the table:

1. **One piece is worth pulling forward on its own: liveness for what is already
   published.** 188 dated rows run to mid-November from 31 hand-entered series, and
   nothing re-checks any of them. That is the exact failure Alex named — a defunct run
   club on the site — and it is live **today**, with no discovery half needed: fetch each
   series' source page on a schedule, flag the ones that stop confirming. Roughly
   **3–4 hours**, reusing the flag machinery, and it is the half that protects the first
   real crowds.
2. **The rubric can be written and measured before the fetching exists**, against the 31
   hand-entered gatherings and the seven that were held. A couple of hours, no new
   machinery, and it removes the largest unknown from the estimate above — which is
   worth doing before deciding whether to move the milestone at all.
